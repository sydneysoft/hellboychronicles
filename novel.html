CHAPTER I

THE FIRST TWO INFINITY STONES

At the edge of Europe the wind came down off the high ground like a warning. Misha stood on a ridge above Sparta and watched distant fires burn along the eastern frontier. They were not campfires. They were the kind of light that ate towns and left only rumor.

Vika stood a few paces behind him, coat drawn tight, hair pulled back as if even the wind had to be organized.

“You can still walk away,” she said.

Misha did not turn. He had walked through many countries. He had slept in stations and borrowed kitchens and rooms that smelled of other people’s lives. He had learned languages the way other men learned routes. None of those places had asked him to stay. Sparta had not asked either. It had simply felt like a place that would notice if he left.

“I have walked through many countries,” he said, “but only one felt like something I could not abandon.”

He closed his hand around a small Spartan emblem worn smooth by travel. The metal was cheap. The meaning was not.

“I choose Sparta.”

Vika watched him the way a person watches a door that has just been locked from the inside.

“Then you must understand what you are choosing. The Dark Empire does not only conquer land. It conquers memory, language, courage, and the will to resist.”

Misha looked toward the dark horizon. Beyond the fires the sky thickened into something that was not weather.

“Then we will defend all of them.”

◆

Far beyond Sparta’s frontier, in a palace built upon fear, the Dark Emperor sat alone at the end of an impossibly long black table. The room was large enough to swallow armies and still feel empty. Generals and bankers stood silently along the walls, as if noise itself had been made illegal.

“Sparta still refuses to kneel,” the Emperor said.

A general stepped forward only as far as protocol allowed.

“Its cities are wounded. Its people are exhausted.”

“Wounds do not guarantee obedience.”

A holographic map unfurled above the table. Sparta appeared as a stubborn island of light surrounded by spreading shadow.

“Destroy their confidence,” the Emperor said. “Make them doubt their friends, their history, and themselves. A country that stops believing in its future will surrender without being asked.”

His face remained half-hidden. That was part of the design. Men who are fully seen can still be argued with. Men who are only suggested become weather.

◆

Beneath an abandoned Spartan library, Vika opened a stone door concealed behind old books. Dust rose like a second language.

“How long have you known about this?” Misha asked.

“Long enough to know that you would not have believed me before tonight.”

They entered a chamber containing a map of the world made from light. Continents hung in the air, not as territory but as living argument. Dozens of colored lights glowed across the map.

“Every country possesses an Infinity Stone,” Vika said. “Each stone contains the deepest power that country learned to master.”

“Science? Courage? Art?”

“Sometimes. A stone can also contain discipline, memory, unity, or the ability to survive.”

As she spoke, many of the lights began to disappear beneath darkness, as if an eclipse were traveling country by country.

“The Emperor is searching for them too.”

Sparta’s own light flickered weakly.

“Why can’t Sparta use its own stone?”

“Because Sparta’s stone is incomplete. Sparta has courage, but courage without technology can be outmatched. It has culture, but culture without confidence can be erased.”

She placed a metal bracelet around Misha’s wrist. It was heavier than it looked, as if it already expected company.

“The stones must be united voluntarily. They cannot be stolen.”

“So I have to convince their guardians.”

“You must prove that you understand what each power means.”

The map shifted. A northern light brightened.

“We begin in the Netherlands.”

◆

The first journey took them to a country that had learned to build order upon water. Canals cut the land into sentences. Wind turbines turned with the patience of machines that had already won an argument with the sea. Trains arrived as if punctuality were a moral quality. Cities looked designed rather than grown.

“Everything seems calm,” Misha said.

“Calm is often the result of invisible work.”

Automated machines moved through a clean industrial district. Nothing hurried. Nothing wasted motion.

“Their power is not size,” Vika said. “It is precision.”

Behind glass, a microchip no larger than a fingernail sat displayed like a relic. Misha leaned closer.

“Something this small can guide a missile, run a hospital, or control an entire factory.”

“And a country that cannot produce advanced technology must depend upon countries that can.”

Somewhere above them, a strange figure watched through a security camera.

“The Spartan has arrived,” said Doctor Hendrik van der Veer.

The laboratory was filled with mirrors, lasers, robotic arms, and unfinished machines that looked like they had opinions. Hendrik appeared wearing mismatched shoes, three pairs of glasses stacked as if one pair were never enough, and a coat covered in formulas.

“Do not touch anything.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“That is what everyone says before destroying ten years of research.”

He examined Misha’s red face tattoo with scientific distaste and faint fascination.

“Interesting design. Terrible for facial-recognition calibration.”

“That may be an advantage.”

Hendrik smiled slightly, which on his face looked like an experiment that had produced an unexpected result.

“Perhaps you are not completely useless.”

The first Infinity Stone was hidden inside a machine capable of writing with light. A blue stone hung suspended within a complex lithography apparatus. Light passed through it and projected microscopic circuits onto the air itself.

“The Stone of Precision,” Hendrik said. “It does not simply create microchips. It teaches a society how to combine science, suppliers, education, patience, and industrial discipline.”

Misha reached toward it. A laser barrier flared into existence.

“You cannot take it because you want better weapons.”

“Sparta needs better defenses.”

“Every aggressor calls his weapon a defense.”

Hendrik activated a test chamber.

“Prove that you understand the difference.”

The simulation dropped Misha into a Spartan city under attack. Sirens. Smoke. A sky that no longer belonged to anyone. Hendrik’s voice came from everywhere.

“You have enough material for only one system.”

Three designs appeared: an offensive missile, an air-defense network, and a civilian infrastructure grid.

“You may strike the enemy’s capital. You may protect Sparta’s sky. Or you may keep its hospitals, water, and communications working.”

Misha studied the options until the noise of the simulation became almost honest.

“The missile may satisfy anger. The shield may stop an attack.”

He selected the civilian infrastructure grid.

“But a country survives through its people.”

The simulation ended. Hendrik regarded him as one might regard a machine that had chosen an unexpected function.

“You sacrificed military advantage.”

“No. I chose the foundation that makes every other defense possible. Microchips should protect medicine, energy, transportation, communication, and education. If Sparta only uses technology to destroy, it will become a reflection of the Empire.”

Hendrik looked at Vika.

“He is impulsive.”

“Extremely.”

“But not foolish.”

“Not always.”

The laser barrier vanished.

The blue stone floated toward Misha’s bracelet as if it had been waiting for a sentence it could accept.

“This stone will not give Sparta a factory overnight,” Hendrik said. “It will show them what must be learned, built, and protected.”

Blue circuits spread across the bracelet.

Precision without purpose is only a more efficient form of chaos.

A vision followed: Spartan engineers building advanced sensors, secure communications, medical devices, and defensive systems. Not miracles. Work. The kind of work that takes years and still arrives too late unless someone begins it now.

“Sparta will no longer be forced to fight with whatever others decide to sell.”

Hendrik had already turned back to his machines.

“Try not to lose it.”

“Has anyone lost an Infinity Stone?”

“That is why I said it.”

◆

Inside the dark palace a blue light appeared on the Emperor’s map.

“The Stone of Precision has awakened,” a general said.

The Emperor studied an image of Misha.

“A wanderer playing the hero.”

“Shall we eliminate him?”

“No. A dead hero becomes a symbol. A corrupted hero becomes a warning.”

He moved a black piece toward Brussels.

“Let him find the next stone. Then teach him to doubt why he wanted it.”

A shadow agent departed without being told twice.

◆

The train to Belgium ran through a landscape that pretended not to know it was being watched. Misha sat with the new weight on his wrist and tried to decide whether the stone had accepted him or merely agreed to travel.

“The first stone was inside one of the most advanced machines in the world,” he said. “Where is the second?”

“In a basement music club.”

He looked at her.

“You are joking.”

“You still think power must look serious.”

Brussels appeared through the window: institutions, arguments, languages stacked on top of one another, and beneath all of it a city that still remembered how to stay awake after midnight.

“A country can have technology and still collapse if its people become tired, isolated, or empty,” Vika said. “The second stone teaches people how to remain alive while they resist.”

The club was underground and already too full. Heat, bodies, bass. Onstage a Black Belgian rapper commanded the room with calm confidence, as if panic were a rumor he refused to subscribe to.

“They told us serious people never dance,” he said. “They told us art was a distraction. But an empire fears the song it cannot silence and the people who refuse to become miserable.”

He noticed Misha the way a man notices a uniform that is trying not to be a uniform.

“Sparta sent a warrior.”

“Sparta sent a traveler.”

“Good. Warriors speak too much about death.”

Backstage he introduced himself as Malik Kabila: artist, producer, community organizer, and guardian of something Misha did not yet understand. He opened a case containing a violet stone that pulsed like a heartbeat.

“The Stone of Rhythm. It holds the ability to remain competent without becoming mechanical. To enjoy beauty without becoming careless.”

He closed the case.

“Sparta knows how to suffer. Does it remember how to live?”

They walked through Brussels at night. Musicians performed beside old buildings as if the stones of the city itself required company.

“When a country is fighting for survival, music can feel unimportant,” Misha said.

“That is exactly when it becomes important. The Dark Empire wants Spartans to believe their only identity is war. If every song, friendship, joke, and dream disappears, what exactly are they defending?”

“Discipline keeps people ready.”

“Yes. But joy gives discipline a reason.”

Malik stopped.

“Your test begins now.”

The shadow agent cut power to the district. The street went black. Phones rose like a field of small, frightened stars.

“Keep the people together,” Malik said, handing Misha a microphone.

“I am not a rapper.”

“Then do not pretend to be one.”

Panic began to spread the way fire spreads when it finds curtains. Misha used the blue stone not as spectacle but as tool: emergency lighting, a thin restoration of communication, enough order to keep the night from becoming a stampede.

“No one leaves alone. Check the person beside you. Keep the exits clear.”

Vika organized the crowd. Musicians began playing without electricity, as if the city had practiced this particular disaster in secret.

“The music is continuing,” Vika said.

“Then we continue with it.”

Misha coordinated security while the crowd sang together. Above them the shadow agent watched, confused that fear had not taken control.

“Why are they still celebrating?”

Malik performed by the light of phones and candles.

“Competence is not the absence of joy. Joy is not the absence of discipline.”

Misha found the agent in the dark and chose the crowd instead of the chase.

“Let him run.”

“You could have caught him,” Vika said.

“And abandoned everyone he endangered.”

Power returned. The audience cheered as if electricity itself had been a guest that had finally remembered its manners. Malik studied Misha with something closer to respect than surprise.

“You used technology without letting the machine become the center of the moment. You remained competent while allowing people to create, sing, and breathe.”

He presented the violet stone. It joined the bracelet. Blue and violet light moved together, not blended so much as in conversation.

A people who remember how to live cannot easily be taught to surrender.

A vision followed: music, theater, education, and community life continuing through Sparta’s darkest days. Not denial. Persistence. The refusal to let occupation become the only story a country is allowed to tell about itself.

“The Empire can damage their buildings,” Misha said. “It must never be allowed to define their soul.”

◆

Before dawn they stood above Brussels. Two stones. Many more to find.

“Precision to build Sparta’s defenses,” Misha said. “Rhythm to protect the spirit of its people.”

Vika looked troubled.

“The stones do not only increase your power. They increase whatever already exists inside you. If you collect them through fear, pride, or revenge, you may save Sparta from one Emperor… and become the next one.”

Misha saw his reflection divided between light and shadow. For a moment he understood the Emperor’s strategy better than he wanted to. Heroes are useful. Heroes who begin to believe they are indispensable are even more useful—to the wrong side.

◆

In the palace the shadow agent made his report.

“The Spartan now possesses two stones.”

“Good,” the Dark Emperor said. “Let him gather them. Every hero eventually believes that only he can save the world.”

◆

Misha and Vika boarded a train toward the next destination. The bracelet was warm now, as if it had opinions about speed.

“Where is the third stone?”

“In a country that mastered memory.”

“And its guardian?”

“Does not believe Sparta deserves to survive.”

The train moved into the sunrise. Far behind them, faint in the clouds, the Dark Empire’s symbol appeared and vanished, like a thought a person is not yet ready to admit they have had.

Misha had chosen Sparta. Now he would have to cross the world, earn the stones, and prove that saving a country does not require becoming the thing that threatens it.

“Then I will give them a reason to believe.”

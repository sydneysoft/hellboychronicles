A UKRAINIAN FOLK TALE · RUKAVYCHKA

THE MITTEN

Once upon a time, long, long ago, in a small wooden village at the edge of a great and ancient forest, there lived an old man, his wife, and their little dog.

It was the deepest part of winter. Snow had fallen for three days and three nights, until the whole world was blanketed in soft, sparkling white. The pine trees wore thick caps of snow on their branches. The river had frozen into a smooth silver ribbon. And the wind sang a quiet, whistling song through the treetops.

One cold morning, the old man's wife looked out the frosty window and said, “Husband, the fire is low. We'll need more wood before nightfall.”

So the old man pulled on his warmest coat, wrapped a scarf twice around his neck, tugged his fur hat down over his ears, and pulled on a pair of thick woolen mittens that his wife had knitted for him with her own hands — soft gray wool, spun from their own sheep, and knitted stitch by careful stitch through the long autumn evenings.

“Come along,” he said to his little dog.

Out they went into the snow, the old man crunching along with his axe over his shoulder, and the dog bounding ahead, sniffing at every snowdrift and barking happily at the little puffs of snow that fell from the branches above.

They walked deep into the forest — past the frozen stream, past the old oak tree split by lightning, past the thicket where the berry bushes slept beneath the snow — until they came to a stand of birch trees, their thin white trunks glowing pale against the gray winter sky.

As the old man swung his arms to keep warm, one of his mittens slipped from his pocket without him noticing at all. It tumbled down, down, down, and landed softly in a snowbank, nestled between two roots, looking for all the world like a small, cozy little house — with a round little door and a warm gray roof.

The old man never noticed. He gathered his firewood, tied it into a bundle, and walked on home with his dog, the two of them disappearing over the snowy hill, their footprints slowly filling in behind them as the snow began to fall once more.

And the mitten lay alone in the quiet, snowy woods, waiting.

Mouse Finds a Home

Not long after, a little gray Mouse came scurrying through the snow. Her whiskers twitched. Her tiny paws left little trail-prints behind her, like stitches sewn into a white blanket. She was cold, and hungry, and her nose was pink from the frosty air, and she was searching, searching, searching for somewhere warm to shelter for the night.

Then she saw it — sitting in the snow, glowing warm and gray in the pale winter light.

“Oh my!” she squeaked, stopping short. “What a snug little house! I wonder who lives here?”

She sniffed at the little round door. No smoke rose from a chimney. No footprints led inside. No one answered her call.

“Squeak, squeak! Is anybody home?”

Silence, except for the whisper of wind through the birches.

“Well then,” said Mouse, puffing out her little chest, “I shall live here myself!”

And she climbed inside, turned around three times the way mice do, curled her tail around her nose, and sighed a happy little sigh. The wool was soft. The mitten was warm. It was, she decided, the very best house a mouse could ask for.

“This will make a perfect home for the winter,” she whispered, and fell fast asleep.

Frog Comes Hopping

Mouse had not slept for long when she heard a sound outside — hop... hop... hop... — soft thumps in the snow, coming closer.

A Frog came hopping along, her green skin cold and damp, her big round eyes blinking sleepily against the winter chill, for frogs, as everyone knows, do not much care for cold weather at all.

She stopped in front of the mitten and blinked at it curiously.

“Ribbit, ribbit! What a cozy little house! I wonder — who lives here?”

Mouse poked her nose out of the little round door. “It's me, Mouse! Who's asking?”

“It's me, Frog! Oh, please, may I come in? My toes are frozen quite through!”

“Of course!” said Mouse. “There's room enough for two. Come in, come in, and warm yourself!”

Frog gave one great hop and landed right inside, tucking her cold, webbed feet beneath her and sighing with relief. “Ahh, much better, ribbit.”

And so now there were two — Mouse and Frog — snuggled warm together inside the little mitten.

Bunny Hops By

Soon, a fluffy white Bunny came bounding through the snowdrifts, her long ears flopping with every hop, her little pink nose twitching at every sound and smell.

She, too, stopped before the little mitten-house, sniffing curiously.

“Who, oh who, lives in this cozy little house?” she asked in her soft, timid voice.

“It's Mouse and Frog!” they called out together. “Come warm your paws with us, Bunny!”

“Oh, thank goodness,” said Bunny, hopping inside and tucking her cold feet under her fluffy white tail. “I've been hopping since sunrise and I can hardly feel my toes at all!”

“Squeeze in beside me,” said Frog kindly, and Bunny did.

Now there were three — Mouse, Frog, and Bunny — all curled together, warm as toast.

Sly Fox Comes Sniffing

Before long, a sleek red Fox came trotting through the trees, her bushy tail swishing behind her like a flame against the white snow, her clever nose sniffing at the little house.

“Well, well,” she said slyly, tilting her head. “What have we here? Who, oh who, lives in this little mitten?”

“Mouse, Frog, and Bunny!” they all called out. “There's room for you too — come in, come in!”

Fox flicked her tail and squeezed herself inside, curling her long red tail around the others like a warm scarf.

“How very snug,” she said, and settled in comfortably.

Now there were four — Mouse, Frog, Bunny, and Fox — all warm together inside the mitten.

Wolf Comes Padding Through

The sun rose higher, though it gave little warmth on that cold winter day. Soon, a big gray Wolf came padding silently through the forest, his paws leaving deep prints in the snow, his breath puffing out in white clouds with every step.

He stopped, sniffed at the little house, and threw back his head. “Awooooo! Who lives in this little mitten?”

Inside, the animals huddled a little closer together. But Mouse called out bravely, “Mouse, Frog, Bunny, and Fox! Squeeze in with us, Wolf, if you promise to be gentle!”

“I promise,” said Wolf, and though it was getting quite snug now, he squeezed himself inside, careful not to step on anyone's toes.

Now there were five — Mouse, Frog, Bunny, Fox, and Wolf — packed together like stitches in a sock.

Boar Comes Grunting

Not long after, a grumbly, tusked Wild Boar came trotting through the underbrush, snorting and snuffling at the snow, his short legs kicking up little white clouds with every step.

“Oink, oink! Who lives in this mitten-house?” he grunted.

“Mouse, Frog, Bunny, Fox, and Wolf!” they all called, squeezed tightly together now. “There's just enough room for one more, if you don't mind a squeeze!”

Boar pushed his way in with a grunt and a wiggle, and the little mitten stretched and strained at the seams to hold them all.

Now there were six — Mouse, Frog, Bunny, Fox, Wolf, and Boar — squeezed in as snug as six stitches on a needle.

The Bear Arrives

Just then, the ground began to tremble — thud... thud... thud... — and the snow shivered from the branches above. Out from the trees lumbered a great big Bear, his fur dusted with snow, his nose sniffing at the cold winter air.

“Grrrowl,” he rumbled, low and deep, like thunder rolling over the hills. “Who lives in this little mitten?”

“Mouse, Frog, Bunny, Fox, Wolf, and Boar!” they all squeaked, and ribbited, and thumped, and yipped, and howled, and grunted together, their voices tumbling over one another.

“Well,” said Bear, scratching his great head, “I am cold too, and I should very much like to live there as well.”

Inside, the animals looked at one another nervously.

“Oh, Bear,” said Mouse in her very smallest voice, “we should love to have you, truly we would — but there simply isn't any room left at all!”

But Bear did not want to be left out in the cold, snowy woods, not one little bit. “I shall just try,” he said, “ever so gently.”

He pushed one big paw in through the little round door.

Then another.

Then he squeezed his big furry shoulders through, huffing and puffing, and the mitten began to stretch...

and stretch...

and stretch...

The wool groaned. The stitches strained. Mouse squeaked, Frog ribbited, Bunny thumped, Fox yipped, Wolf howled, and Boar oinked, all squashed together tighter and tighter as Bear pushed his way inside —

POP!

The mitten burst apart at every seam, and Mouse, Frog, Bunny, Fox, Wolf, Boar, and Bear all went tumbling out into the soft white snow in one great, laughing, squeaking, growling, snorting heap — arms and paws and ears and tails all tangled together!

They rolled in the snow, blinking up at the gray winter sky, and for a moment, nobody moved at all.

The Little Dog Returns

Just then, from over the snowy hill, came a sound: “Woof! Woof! Woof!”

Back at the cottage, the old man's wife had been mending his mittens by the fire when she noticed — only one!

“Husband,” she said, “where is your other mitten?”

The old man patted his pockets. “Why, I must have dropped it in the woods!”

So the little dog, wagging his tail eagerly, dashed back out into the forest to search for it, following the old scent all the way to the stand of birch trees.

When the animals heard the barking growing closer — “Woof! Woof! Woof!” — they scattered in every direction at once! Mouse dashed into a hollow log. Frog hopped into a snowbank and vanished with a plop. Bunny bounded off into the bushes, a streak of white against white. Fox slipped away silently between the birch trunks. Wolf loped off into the shadows of the deep forest. Boar trotted grumbling back into the thicket. And Bear, who was in no hurry at all, simply ambled off toward his den for a long winter's nap, yawning as he went.

The little dog trotted up to the burst and empty mitten lying in the snow, sniffed it all over, and picked it up gently in his mouth. Then, wagging his tail proudly, he ran back over the hill to find the old man.

“Well done!” said the old man, taking the torn mitten and turning it over in his hands. “Though I'm afraid your mother will have some mending to do tonight.”

And that evening, by the warm firelight, the old man's wife took out her needle and thread, and stitched the little mitten back together again, good as new — ready for the next cold winter morning.

And So It Goes...

And that is the story of how one small woolen mitten, lost in the snow, became a warm little home for seven forest friends — a mouse, a frog, a bunny, a fox, a wolf, a boar, and a bear — if only for a little while, on one cold winter's day in the great forest.

Squeak, ribbit, thump, yip, howl, oink, and growl —
that's the story of the mitten, one and all!
